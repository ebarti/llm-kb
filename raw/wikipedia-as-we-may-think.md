# Vannevar Bush's "As We May Think" (1945)

Published in The Atlantic in July 1945, this visionary essay anticipated many aspects of the information society. Republished in abridged form in Life Magazine, September 1945.

## Context
Scientists who had focused on weapons development during WWII needed to redirect toward peaceful purposes. Bush articulated concern that scientific efforts had emphasized destruction rather than understanding.

## The Memex Concept
Bush proposed the "memex" (memory + index) — a desk-sized electromechanical device for storing and accessing information using microfilm technology.

### Associative Indexing
The revolutionary feature: rather than hierarchical, tree-like classification systems, users could create connections between any items, forming "trails." These associative pathways mirrored how human memory actually operates — through connection and relation rather than alphabetical or numerical filing.

### Key Quote
"Wholly new forms of encyclopedias will appear, ready-made with a mesh of associative trails running through them."

## Technological Predictions
The essay presciently anticipated:
- Hypertext and hyperlinks
- Personal computers
- The Internet and World Wide Web
- Speech recognition
- Online encyclopedias like Wikipedia

## The Information Explosion
Bush diagnosed the overwhelming accumulation of scientific knowledge that researchers could no longer efficiently manage. Advocated that scientists should "delegate routine mathematics to machines" to focus on creative, intuitive work.

## Influence on Computing Pioneers
- **Doug Engelbart**: Encountered the essay shortly after publication, kept memex principles central to his research, eventually developed the mouse, word processor, and hyperlink
- **Ted Nelson**: Built on Bush's associative trails concept to develop hypertext theory
- **Tim Berners-Lee**: Web inherits the spirit of associative linking

## Legacy
Recognized as describing "a microcosm of the information society." Established information science as a key discipline. The challenges of information overload Bush identified remain relevant today across education, healthcare, government, and professional domains.
