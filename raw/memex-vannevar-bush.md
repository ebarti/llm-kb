# Memex: Concept, History, and Legacy

## Core Concept

The memex (portmanteau of "memory" and "index") is a hypothetical electromechanical device conceptualized by Vannevar Bush in his 1945 article "As We May Think," published in The Atlantic. Bush envisioned it as a personal information management system where individuals could "compress and store all of their books, records, and communications" in a device that functioned as "an enlarged intimate supplement to his memory."

## Technical Design

Bush's proposed device combined electromechanical controls with microfilm technology integrated into a large desk:
- **Storage**: Microfilm reels housed within the desk, removable by users
- **Access mechanism**: Electric photocells reading coded symbols on microfilm frames
- **Display**: Slanting translucent screens for viewing projected material
- **Input**: A transparent platen allowing users to photograph notes and documents directly onto microfilm

## Associative Trails

The memex's distinguishing feature was its capacity for "associative trails" — connections mimicking human mental association. Users could create linear sequences of microfilm frames through arbitrary linking, with personal comments and side trails enabling knowledge exploration analogous to human thought processes rather than traditional linear indexing.

## Influence on Computing History

### Douglas Engelbart
After reading "As We May Think" in 1945, Engelbart recalled becoming "infected" with the concept of extending human knowledge interaction. By 1962-1963, Engelbart developed designs updating Bush's microfilm desk concept toward personal computers with electronic displays and pointing devices. His 1968 "Mother of All Demos" demonstrated hypertext, the mouse, and collaborative computing.

### Ted Nelson
Coined "hypertext" in 1965, explicitly quoting Bush's memex concept. Nelson and Andries van Dam implemented the Hypertext Editing System (HES) in 1968. Nelson defined hypertext as "non-sequential writing with reader-controlled links." His Xanadu project attempted to build a global hypertext system.

### Tim Berners-Lee
Independently developed ENQUIRE software at CERN in 1980 without prior knowledge of Bush, Engelbart, or Nelson's work. However, as Berners-Lee refined his concepts, these predecessors' contributions validated his eventual 1989 invention of the World Wide Web.

### J.C.R. Licklider
Dedicated his 1965 book "Libraries of the Future" to Bush, having encountered memex and trail concepts before reading the original article.

## Later Developments

### Memex II (1959)
Bush proposed improvements emphasizing electronic distribution. He imagined professional societies maintaining "master memex" systems where papers would arrive via tape or telephone downloads, "intimately interconnected by trails."

### Memex Revisited (1967)
Bush acknowledged technological advances (transistors, cathode ray tubes, magnetic tape, solid-state miniaturization) supporting his vision. He emphasized that "selection, in the broad sense, is still a stone adze," highlighting enduring challenges in information retrieval beyond speed alone.

## Contemporary Applications

- **DARPA Memex Program** (2014): Launched to combat human trafficking on the dark web, explicitly citing Bush's device. Released as open-source software.
- **Microsoft's MyLifeBits** (2003): Attempted fulfilling Bush's vision through life-logging research.

## Enduring Impact

The memex concept influenced development of early hypertext systems and personal knowledge base software. While Bush's specific implementation remained theoretical, his foundational ideas about associative information organization, user-controlled navigation, and knowledge sharing networks proved prescient for digital media evolution.
