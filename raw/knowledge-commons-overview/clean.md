# Knowledge Commons

## Definition

Knowledge commons refers to "information, data, and content that is collectively owned and managed by a community of users," particularly online. Digital resources are non-subtractible — multiple users can access the same materials without diminishing quantity or quality.

## Key Property

Unlike natural commons, in the knowledge commons "use by one not only does not reduce the resource available to the other but in fact enhances it adding to its value."

## Intellectual Traditions

1. **European tradition**: Resists the "enclosure of the intangible commons of the mind" threatened by expanding intellectual property rights
2. **American tradition**: Emphasizes commons as shared spaces supporting free speech and democratic practices

## Key Theorists

**Charlotte Hess and Elinor Ostrom** provided foundational research distinguishing "information" from "knowledge" as shared resources. Ostrom emphasized that knowledge cannot become shared without "a complex set of institutions and practices" enabling recipients to internalize information.

## Examples

- **Educational**: MIT OpenCourseWare, open educational resources
- **Digital media**: Wikipedia, Creative Commons-licensed content
- **Scientific**: Public Library of Science, Science Commons
- **Software**: Open-source projects, free software
- **Design**: Open design movements, collaborative architecture

## Governance: Copyleft Licensing

Copyleft licenses institutionalize knowledge commons by granting rights to study, use, modify, and redistribute — with the condition that derivative works remain in the commons. Examples: GNU licenses (GPL, LGPL, GFDL), Creative Commons share-alike.

## Production Models

Development driven by "collective intelligence" and "wisdom of crowds," relating to knowledge communism (Robert K. Merton): contributors surrender intellectual property rights in exchange for recognition and esteem.

## Contemporary Challenges

UNESCO identifies the structure of governance as crucial: it determines how much of the knowledge stored in digital form belongs to the commons and how much remains in private enclosures. A different governance system is needed to shift the power balance in data and AI, moving beyond "open data" toward building robust and sustainable digital commons ecosystems.
