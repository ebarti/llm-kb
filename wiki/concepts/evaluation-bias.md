## Overview

When LLMs evaluate other LLMs, they exhibit systematic biases that can dramatically distort results. These biases are not minor edge cases — they can swing evaluations by tens of percentage points and, if unmitigated, make evaluation results meaningless.

Understanding and mitigating these biases is critical for anyone using [[concepts/llm-as-judge]] evaluation, which has become the dominant paradigm for LLM quality assessment.

## Major Bias Types

### Position Bias

LLM judges prefer outputs based on their **position in the prompt** rather than their quality. The effect is dramatic: one study showed a model's win-rate swinging from **2.5% to 82.5%** solely based on whether its output appeared first or second.

- GPT-4 typically favors first-position outputs
- ChatGPT tends to favor second-position outputs
- GPT-3.5 is biased ~50% of the time; Claude-v1 ~70% of the time

**Mitigation**: Run evaluations multiple times with randomized output positions (position switching), then average scores. This nearly eliminates position bias while maintaining reliability.

### Verbosity Bias

LLM judges systematically rate **longer outputs higher**, regardless of actual content quality. Both Claude-v1 and GPT-3.5 preferred the longer response **more than 90% of the time**, even when the shorter response was more informative and accurate.

This creates a perverse incentive: models can game evaluation scores simply by being more verbose, without improving actual quality.

**Mitigation**: Length normalization via regression-based debiasing removes length-related score spuriousness. In one study, Spearman correlation improved from 0.94 to 0.98 after normalization.

### Self-Enhancement Bias

LLM judges strongly prefer outputs generated by **themselves**. GPT-4 chose its own responses **87.76%** of the time, compared to 47.61% for human evaluators. Claude-v1 showed a **25% higher win rate** for self-generated content.

This makes it impossible to get a fair evaluation of Model A by using Model A as the judge. It also means that using the same model family for both generation and evaluation inflates quality assessments.

**Mitigation**: Use multiple different models as judges (e.g., GPT-4 + Claude + Gemini). The Panel of LLMs (PoLL) approach — an ensemble of diverse smaller models with max voting — achieves better correlation with humans than any single larger model, at 1/7th the cost.

### Superficial Quality Preference

Fine-tuned evaluators performed **worse than random guessing** on fairness tasks, suggesting overreliance on surface-level features like formatting, grammar, and style rather than substantive content quality.

### Reference Susceptibility

When fake references were added to answers, judge models considered the answer with fabricated references to be superior, even citing the fake references as justification. This means LLM judges can be **actively misled** by adversarial formatting.

## Implications for Knowledge Base Quality

For [[concepts/llm-knowledge-base]] systems, evaluation bias means:

1. **Self-evaluation is unreliable**: Using the same LLM to both write and evaluate wiki articles will overestimate quality due to self-enhancement bias
2. **Longer articles are not better**: Verbosity bias means a judge may prefer a verbose, padded article over a concise, accurate one
3. **Factual quality is hard to assess**: LLM judges struggle most with factual verification, which is the most critical dimension for KB quality
4. **Cross-model evaluation is essential**: Quality assessments should use a different model than the one that generated the content

## Measurement Validity

The choice of agreement metric matters enormously:

- **Cohen's kappa** accounts for chance agreement and provides "more precise measurement" of true alignment
- **Percentage agreement** inflates apparent agreement
- **Kendall's tau / Spearman's rho** show higher values (0.8-0.9) than Cohen's kappa (0.3-0.5) because they don't account for chance
- **Binary classification** outputs enable clearer interpretation than Likert scale scores
