## Overview

The Brussels Effect refers to the European Union's ability to set global regulatory standards through market power alone. When the EU adopts stringent regulations, multinational companies often implement those standards globally rather than maintaining separate systems for different jurisdictions. GDPR's influence on global data privacy is the canonical example.

## Application to AI

The [[entities/eu-ai-act]]'s architects explicitly aim to replicate GDPR's success. With full enforcement beginning August 2026 and penalties up to 7% of global revenue, the Act creates strong incentives for compliance. If companies like Microsoft, Google, and Meta adopt EU-compliant AI practices globally, the Brussels Effect would make EU standards the de facto worldwide norm.

**Evidence of early adoption**: Microsoft has already adopted a "Compliance-by-Design" strategy, updating its Microsoft Purview platform to automate conformity assessments ([[sources/cfr-how-2026-decides-future-of-ai]]).

## Challenges

Unlike data privacy, AI regulation faces distinct barriers to the Brussels Effect:
- The US under the Trump administration is actively pursuing deregulation, creating a competing model
- China's "develop hard, control tight" approach offers an alternative regulatory framework
- AI capability development may concentrate in jurisdictions with lighter regulation
- The EU's regulatory agility is questioned — ChatGPT emerged after the Act's 2021 proposal
