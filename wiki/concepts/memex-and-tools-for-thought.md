## Overview

"Tools for thought" is a phrase that captures an 80-year lineage of systems designed to augment human intellectual capability — from [[entities/vannevar-bush]]'s hypothetical memex (1945) through Douglas Engelbart's interactive computing, Ted Nelson's hypertext, Tim Berners-Lee's World Wide Web, and into today's networked note-taking tools and [[concepts/llm-knowledge-base]] systems.

The unifying idea across this lineage: human knowledge is associative, not hierarchical, and our tools should reflect this. Every generation has attempted to build systems that make associative connections computationally navigable.

## The Memex (1945)

Vannevar Bush's "As We May Think" (The Atlantic, July 1945) proposed the memex — a desk-sized device storing an individual's entire library on microfilm with the ability to create **associative trails**: personal paths through knowledge linking related documents. Bush argued that alphabetical and hierarchical indexing were cognitively unnatural; the memex would let users forge connections that mirrored their own thinking patterns.

The memex's most prescient features were:
- Personal annotation and commentary on stored documents
- User-created trails linking related items (proto-hyperlinks)
- The idea that these trails could be shared with others
- Recognition that the fundamental problem is not storage but **selection** — finding relevant knowledge when needed

Bush revisited the concept twice: Memex II (1959) envisioned networked knowledge sharing among professional societies, and Memex Revisited (1967) acknowledged that despite hardware advances, "selection, in the broad sense, is still a stone adze."

## The Influence Chain

**Douglas Engelbart** (1962-1968): Read Bush's essay in 1945, became "infected" with extending human knowledge interaction. Developed the NLS (oN-Line System), demonstrating the mouse, hypertext, real-time collaboration, and video conferencing in the 1968 "Mother of All Demos." His stated goal: "augmenting human intellect."

**Ted Nelson** (1965): Coined "hypertext" explicitly referencing Bush's associative trails. Proposed Xanadu — a global hypertext system with bidirectional links, transclusion (embedding documents within documents), and micropayment for creators. Xanadu was never completed but profoundly influenced all subsequent hypertext systems.

**Tim Berners-Lee** (1989): Developed the World Wide Web at CERN. While he worked independently of Bush/Engelbart/Nelson initially, their work validated his approach. The Web implemented a subset of the memex vision: associative linking across networked documents, though without bidirectional links or personal trail-making.

## The Modern Expression

Today's PKM tools complete the circle back to Bush's original vision:

| Memex Feature (1945) | Modern Implementation |
|----------------------|----------------------|
| Store personal library | Local markdown vault (Obsidian, Logseq) |
| Associative trails | Bidirectional links, wikilinks |
| Personal annotation | Evergreen notes, Zettelkasten cards |
| Share trails with others | Digital gardens, public note systems |
| Intelligent selection | LLM-powered search and synthesis |

The one capability Bush imagined that has only recently become feasible is **intelligent selection** — using AI to surface relevant knowledge from vast personal stores. This is precisely what [[concepts/llm-knowledge-base]] systems and [[concepts/agentic-knowledge-management]] now provide, arguably fulfilling Bush's 80-year-old vision more completely than any prior system.

## The Persistent Problem

Bush's 1967 observation — "selection, in the broad sense, is still a stone adze" — remains the central challenge. We can store everything, link anything, and search by keyword. But the ability to **find the right knowledge at the right time** — to surface unexpected but relevant connections, to synthesize across sources, to know what you don't know — has only begun to be addressed by LLMs and knowledge graph systems.
