## Overview

Evergreen notes are [[entities/andy-matuschak]]'s framework for notes that are "written and organized to evolve, contribute, and accumulate over time, across projects." Unlike transient notes (meeting minutes, to-do lists, reading highlights), evergreen notes are designed to be revisited, revised, and connected across years and domains. The core insight is that "better note-taking misses the point; what matters is better thinking."

## Five Principles

1. **Evergreen notes should be atomic**: Small enough to complete in under 30 minutes, each capturing a single self-contained idea. This enables flexible recombination and creates natural checkpoints of solid intellectual ground.

2. **Evergreen notes should be concept-oriented**: Organized by the concept they discuss, not the source that spawned them. A note about "confirmation bias" should synthesize across all sources, not live in a folder for a specific book.

3. **Evergreen notes should be densely linked**: Rich associative networks promote serendipitous discovery and cross-domain synthesis. The links are not organizational — they are intellectual, revealing how ideas relate.

4. **Prefer associative ontologies to hierarchical taxonomies**: Rigid folder hierarchies force single classification. Associative networks allow ideas to exist in multiple contexts simultaneously, reflecting knowledge's true structure.

5. **Write notes for yourself, not for an audience**: Personal clarity over external presentation. This removes the friction of performance and enables authentic exploration of half-formed ideas.

## Relationship to Zettelkasten

Matuschak's practice is "heavily inspired by [[entities/niklas-luhmann]]'s [[concepts/zettelkasten]]" but he deliberately uses a distinct term to explore variations without inheriting the cultural baggage of Zettelkasten discourse. Key similarities include atomicity, dense linking, and the emphasis on personal thinking. Key differences include Matuschak's greater emphasis on concept-orientation (versus Luhmann's emphasis on branching thought paths) and his engagement with public note-sharing (versus Luhmann's purely private system).

## Relationship to Spaced Repetition

Matuschak explicitly distinguishes evergreen notes from [[concepts/spaced-repetition]] systems. The former builds ideas; the latter aids retention. Existing spaced repetition tools "discourage the incremental synthesis that evergreen notes require." However, the regular maintenance and revisiting of evergreen notes approximates spaced repetition organically — you encounter old ideas when linking new ones, refreshing and reconsidering them.

## The Compounding Effect

The key property of evergreen notes is that intellectual work compounds rather than dissipates. In traditional workflows, thinking done for one project evaporates when the project ends. With evergreen notes, every insight is captured in durable form and linked into the growing network, where it can be discovered and recombined in future contexts.

Matuschak proposes that "the number of evergreen notes written per day" is a more meaningful productivity metric for knowledge workers than hours worked, meetings attended, or emails sent.
