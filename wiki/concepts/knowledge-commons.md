## Overview

Knowledge commons refers to information, data, and content collectively owned and managed by communities. Unlike natural commons (fisheries, forests), knowledge commons are **non-subtractible**: use by one person does not reduce availability to others and typically **enhances** value for everyone. Wikipedia exemplifies this: each edit adds value accessible to all, and the resource grows rather than depletes through use.

The concept draws on [[entities/elinor-ostrom]]'s Nobel Prize-winning work on commons governance, adapted to knowledge by Charlotte Hess. Their key insight: information does not automatically become shared knowledge — it requires institutional infrastructure (governance rules, licensing, community norms) to function as a true commons.

## Key Ideas

### The Non-Subtractibility Advantage

Physical commons face the "tragedy of the commons" — overuse depletes the resource. Knowledge commons face the opposite dynamic: **underuse** and **enclosure** are the threats. When knowledge is paywalled, patented, or hoarded, collective value is destroyed. When it is shared, value multiplies. This is why open-access movements, Creative Commons licensing, and copyleft represent rational collective strategy, not charity.

### Two Intellectual Traditions

1. **European**: Resists "enclosure of the intangible commons of the mind" — expanding IP rights that privatize shared knowledge
2. **American**: Commons as democratic infrastructure supporting free speech and collective governance

### Governance Mechanisms

- **Copyleft licensing**: GPL, Creative Commons share-alike — derivative works must remain in the commons
- **Community norms**: Wikipedia's notability, verifiability, and NPOV policies
- **Institutional oversight**: Wikimedia Foundation, Apache Foundation, etc.
- **Recognition incentives**: Contributors surrender IP for recognition and esteem (Merton's "knowledge communism")

### Major Knowledge Commons

| Commons | Type | Scale | Governance |
|---------|------|-------|------------|
| Wikipedia | Encyclopedic | 60M+ articles | CC BY-SA, community norms |
| Wikidata | Structured data | 100M+ items | CC0, community curation |
| MIT OpenCourseWare | Educational | 2,500+ courses | CC BY-NC-SA |
| Open-source software | Code | Millions of projects | Various OSS licenses |
| arXiv | Scientific | 2M+ papers | Author copyright, open access |
| Public Library of Science | Scientific | Thousands of papers | CC BY |

## The AI Challenge

AI creates an unprecedented crisis for knowledge commons ([[sources/cip-generative-ai-digital-commons]]):

**The Parasitism Paradox**: AI models are trained on commons data ("painstakingly edited Wikipedia, millennia's worth of books, billions of Reddit comments") but rarely return value. The value flows one way: from public contributors to private companies.

**Seven Risks** identified by Huang and Siddarth:
1. Information pollution — AI generates faster than communities verify
2. Democratic erosion — design decisions by small unaccountable teams
3. Content homogenization — similar models produce uniform outputs
4. Reduced contributions — creators stop contributing when displaced
5. Economic concentration — few companies control commons-derived infrastructure
6. Labor precarity — automation without worker protection
7. Unpredictable emergent risks

**The Doom Loop**: Information pollution degrades commons quality, content homogenization reduces diversity, reduced contributions shrink the training data for future models, and economic concentration removes governance from communities.

**Wikipedia-specific impacts** ([[sources/ai-in-wikimedia-projects]]): 8% visitor decrease in 2025, Wikimedia Foundation forced to introduce paid APIs, AI companies scraping without attribution or compensation.

### Governance Proposals

The [[entities/collective-intelligence-project]] proposes three responses:
1. **Monitoring consortia** — multi-stakeholder standards and audit frameworks
2. **Commons data contributions** — AI companies contribute datasets back to public repositories
3. **Data-based governance** — training data contributors receive ownership stakes or governance rights
