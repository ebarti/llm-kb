## Overview

Digital commons governance encompasses the rules, norms, institutions, and technical mechanisms that sustain shared knowledge resources. Drawing on [[entities/elinor-ostrom]]'s principles for governing natural commons, digital commons governance addresses unique challenges: non-subtractible resources, global scale, and the new threat of AI-driven value extraction.

## Key Ideas

### Ostrom's Framework Applied to Knowledge

Ostrom's eight design principles for sustainable commons governance translate to digital knowledge:

1. **Clear boundaries**: Who is a community member? (Wikipedia: registered editors with established accounts)
2. **Proportional rules**: Rules match local conditions (Wikipedia: different policies per language edition)
3. **Collective choice**: Those affected by rules participate in making them (Wikipedia: community-driven policy)
4. **Monitoring**: Observable compliance (Wikipedia: public edit histories, watchlists)
5. **Graduated sanctions**: Escalating responses to violations (Wikipedia: warnings, blocks, bans)
6. **Conflict resolution**: Accessible dispute mechanisms (Wikipedia: talk pages, mediation, arbitration)
7. **Minimal state interference**: External authorities respect community governance
8. **Nested enterprises**: Multi-level governance for large-scale commons (Wikipedia: local → language → Foundation)

### Current Governance Mechanisms

- **Licensing**: Copyleft (GPL, CC BY-SA) and permissive (MIT, CC0) licenses
- **Community norms**: Evolved social contracts (Wikipedia's five pillars, open-source community standards)
- **Foundation oversight**: Non-profit stewardship (Wikimedia, Apache, Linux Foundation)
- **Technical enforcement**: Access controls, automated policy checks, edit filters

### The AI Governance Gap

Existing governance mechanisms were designed for human-to-human knowledge sharing. AI introduces challenges they were not built to handle:

1. **Scale mismatch**: AI can extract value from the entire commons simultaneously; governance was designed for individual human contributions
2. **Attribution failure**: AI training on commons data breaks the attribution chain that licenses depend on
3. **Generative pollution**: AI outputs fed back into commons overwhelm verification capacity
4. **Incentive erosion**: When AI can generate content, human contributors' recognition incentive weakens

### CIP's Three Governance Proposals

The [[entities/collective-intelligence-project]] proposes ([[sources/cip-generative-ai-digital-commons]]):

1. **Monitoring & Standards Consortia**: Multi-stakeholder organizations establishing transparency standards, audit frameworks, and AI content detection tools
2. **Commons Data Contributions**: AI companies contribute high-quality datasets back to public repositories, creating reciprocal value flows
3. **Data-Based Governance Structures**: Training data contributors receive ownership stakes or governance rights in AI systems — the most structurally radical proposal

### UNESCO's Position

UNESCO identifies the governance structure as determinative: it decides "how much of the knowledge stored in digital form belongs to the commons and how much will remain in private enclosures." They advocate moving beyond "open data" rhetoric toward sustainable digital commons ecosystems.
