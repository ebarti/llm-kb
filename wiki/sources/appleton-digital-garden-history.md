## Key Points

- Digital gardens originated with Mark Bernstein's 1998 "Hypertext Gardens" and were redefined by Mike Caufield's 2015 "The Garden and the Stream" keynote
- Caufield's key distinction: Streams (ephemeral, timeline-based) vs. Gardens (richly linked, accumulating landscapes)
- Six core patterns: topography over timelines, continuous growth, imperfection/learning-in-public, playful experimentation, intercropping/content diversity, independent ownership
- Epistemic status markers (seedling/budding/evergreen) signal knowledge maturity transparently
- The movement emerged alongside IndieWeb, Roam Research users, BASB community, and Learn in Public advocates
- Represents a philosophical rejection of the "Chronological Sort Era" initiated by blogging platforms

## Detailed Summary

Maggie Appleton's comprehensive history traces the digital garden concept through three distinct eras. The term first appeared in Mark Bernstein's 1998 "Hypertext Gardens," which addressed navigation problems in hypertext systems. From 2007-2011, "digital gardening" merely meant system maintenance. The pivotal moment came in 2015 when Mike Caufield delivered "The Garden and the Stream: a Technopastoral," establishing the philosophical framework that defines the movement today.

Caufield's distinction is profound: the **Stream** (Twitter, email, blogs) presents ephemeral, reverse-chronological content that flows past you. The **Garden** is the web as topology — richly linked, iteratively cultivated, and explored non-linearly. The garden philosophy rejects the assumption that content must be "finished" before publishing, that chronological order is natural, or that web content should be disposable.

The 2018-2020 period saw the movement crystallize through contributions from Tom Critchlow (adding "campfires" to the metaphor), Joel Hooks ("My blog is a digital garden, not a blog"), Amy Hoy (historical analysis of how Moveable Type standardized reverse-chronological publishing), and Shaun Wang (formalizing community standards). By late 2020, MIT Technology Review had legitimized the concept.

Appleton identifies six patterns that distinguish digital gardens from blogs. Perhaps most distinctive is **learning in public** — publishing half-finished thoughts with epistemic status markers that signal how developed an idea is. This transparency about knowledge maturity is the opposite of performative expertise.
