## Key Points

- Galton (1906): 800 guesses, median within 1% of actual weight
- Four conditions: diversity, independence, decentralization, aggregation capability
- Page's Diversity Prediction Theorem: collective error = average individual error minus diversity
- "Surprisingly popular" algorithm reduces errors 21.3% vs. simple majority
- Limitations: systematic biases, social influence, strategic voting, digital platform distortions

## Detailed Summary

The [[concepts/wisdom-of-crowds]] concept traces from Aristotle through Francis Galton's 1906 ox-weighing experiment to modern digital platforms. The core insight: aggregated independent judgments outperform individual experts because random individual errors cancel while shared information reinforces.

Scott E. Page's **Diversity Prediction Theorem** formalizes this: collective squared error equals average individual squared error minus the group's predictive diversity. This means diversity is not just nice to have — it is mathematically essential to crowd accuracy. A homogeneous crowd, no matter how expert, cannot achieve wisdom-of-crowds effects.

The conditions for success (diversity, independence, decentralization, aggregation) map directly to design requirements for [[concepts/collaborative-knowledge-building]] systems. Wikipedia succeeds partly because it satisfies all four: diverse editors, largely independent contributions, no central authority, and structured aggregation through editorial processes.

Modern innovations include MIT's **"surprisingly popular" method** — asking people both what they believe AND what they think others believe, then selecting answers that are more popular than expected. This cleverly extracts private knowledge from crowds.

Digital-era challenges include demographic biases, power-law activity distributions (highly active users dominate), and bot influence — all of which undermine the independence and diversity conditions.
