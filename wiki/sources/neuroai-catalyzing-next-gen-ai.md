## Key Points

- Modern AI owes a deep historical debt to neuroscience — CNNs from visual cortex, RL from dopamine signals, attention from cognitive science
- Current AI lacks capabilities the brain handles effortlessly: few-shot learning, robust generalization, energy efficiency, common-sense reasoning
- Proposes a comprehensive NeuroAI research agenda studying biological intelligence principles for AI
- Advocates bidirectional exchange: neuroscience inspires AI, AI tools accelerate neuroscience

## Detailed Summary

The paper, published in Nature Communications by Zador, Escola, Richards, and a large consortium, argues that the next breakthroughs in AI will come not just from scaling existing architectures but from deeply engaging with how biological brains solve intelligence.

The NeuroAI research agenda covers six core areas: (1) how the brain achieves sample-efficient learning through [[concepts/complementary-learning-systems]] involving the hippocampus and cortex; (2) the role of embodiment and sensorimotor interaction; (3) how biological circuits implement attention and memory; (4) the principles behind neural efficiency and [[concepts/sparse-coding]]; (5) how brains handle [[concepts/continual-learning]] without catastrophic forgetting; and (6) the architecture of biological reward and motivation systems.

Key neuroscience principles identified for AI include synaptic plasticity rules beyond backpropagation (Hebbian learning, spike-timing-dependent plasticity), hierarchical [[concepts/predictive-coding]], [[concepts/complementary-learning-systems]], sparse distributed representations, embodied cognition, and neuromodulation via global signals like dopamine and serotonin.
