## Key Points

- **Physical AI** is defined as AI systems that autonomously perceive, reason about, and interact with the physical world in real time -- distinct from traditional preprogrammed robots
- [[concepts/vision-language-action-models]] integrate computer vision, NLP, and motor control as the core enabling technology
- Current real-world deployments span autonomous vehicles (Waymo: 10M+ rides), warehousing (Amazon: 1M robots deployed), manufacturing (BMW), healthcare (GE HealthCare), and drones
- UBS projects 2M humanoids in workplaces by 2035, 300M by 2050; TAM of $30-50B by 2035 growing to $1.4-1.7T by 2050
- Material costs already dropping: ~$35K (2025), projected $13-17K next decade (Bank of America)
- The convergence of [[concepts/agentic-ai]] with [[concepts/physical-ai]] over the next decade will produce robots whose "brains" are agentic AIs

## Detailed Summary

Deloitte's 2026 Tech Trends report identifies physical AI as one of the defining technology themes. The report distinguishes physical AI from traditional automation: "Automation alone doesn't make them revolutionary; rather, it's their capacity to bridge the gap between digital intelligence and the physical world."

The technology stack rests on three pillars: (1) [[concepts/vision-language-action-models]] that adapt LLM training paradigms to physical world data, (2) onboard neural processing units enabling low-latency inference without cloud dependency, and (3) advances in sensors, actuators, spatial computing, and battery technology.

Training combines [[concepts/reinforcement-learning]] (trial-and-error), [[concepts/imitation-learning]] (mimicking demonstrations), and blended sim-to-real approaches. The persistent [[concepts/sim-to-real-transfer]] gap remains a primary barrier: "A robot might learn to grab something in simulation, but when it enters physical space, it's not a one-to-one match."

The report documents extensive current deployments: [[entities/waymo]] has completed over 10 million paid robotaxi rides; Aurora Innovation launched commercial Dallas-Houston freight; Amazon's DeepFleet AI coordinates millions of warehouse robots (10% efficiency improvement); BMW tests humanoid robots alongside traditional automation; GE HealthCare is building autonomous diagnostic imaging systems.

The [[concepts/humanoid-robots]] market is entering a critical inflection point. [[entities/tesla-optimus]] plans 50,000 units in 2026 at $20-30K; [[entities/boston-dynamics]] targets commercial Atlas launch at $140-150K. Humanoid form factor wins because it can navigate existing human infrastructure without modification.

Six implementation barriers are documented: sim-to-real gap, AI safety in physical contexts (error cascading), regulatory fragmentation, human acceptance, cybersecurity (new attack surfaces bridging digital and physical), and fleet orchestration across heterogeneous vendor ecosystems.

Expert Ayanna Howard (Ohio State) insists: "There should always be a human in the loop somewhere. Always." Jonathan Hurst (Agility Robotics) credits success to identifying hundreds of use cases where humanoid form genuinely matched requirements.
