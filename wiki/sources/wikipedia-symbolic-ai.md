## Key Points
- Symbolic AI dominated from 1950s through 1980s, with two boom-bust cycles (AI summers/winters)
- "Neats" (formal logic, Stanford/CMU) vs. "scruffies" (ad hoc, MIT) was the field's internal divide
- Expert systems boom (1978-1987) was the commercial peak: "in the knowledge lies the power"
- Deep learning breakthrough (~2012) shifted dominance to connectionist approaches
- Modern consensus: complementary (Kahneman's System 1/System 2 analogy)
- Six neuro-symbolic integration architectures provide a taxonomy for hybrid approaches

## Detailed Summary

The article traces [[concepts/symbolic-ai]] through five eras. The first AI summer (1948-1966) saw Logic Theorist prove theorems from Principia Mathematica and GPS demonstrate domain-independent problem solving. The first AI winter (1967-1977) followed unfulfilled promises, with the Lighthill Report declaring AI couldn't scale.

The second AI summer (1978-1987) was driven by [[concepts/expert-systems]] — DENDRAL, MYCIN, XCON proved commercial viability. The second AI winter (1988-1993) came when expert systems proved costly and brittle.

A rigorous foundations period (1993-2011) introduced probabilistic methods (Bayesian Networks, HMMs) and automated knowledge acquisition (Meta-DENDRAL, decision trees, inductive logic programming). The deep learning era (2011-present) brought GPU-accelerated neural networks to dominance.

The [[concepts/symbolic-vs-connectionist]] debate is the field's defining tension. The article identifies Kahneman's System 1 (fast pattern recognition = deep learning) vs. System 2 (deliberate reasoning = symbolic AI) as the modern framing.

Six [[concepts/neural-symbolic-integration]] architectures are catalogued, from language models using symbolic tokens (BERT, GPT-3) to neural models calling symbolic engines.

Key programming languages: LISP (1958, American AI) and Prolog (European AI). KR methods: semantic networks, frames (Minsky), scripts (Schank), ontologies (WordNet, YAGO), description logic (OWL).
