## Key Points
- KR&R is one of AI's foundational subfields, tracing back to Newell and Simon's General Problem Solver (1959)
- The field progressed through distinct phases: logic (1960s), frames (1970s), expert systems (1980s), ontologies (1990s-2000s)
- The expressivity-tractability trade-off remains the central design tension
- Modern machine learning (including transformers) can itself be viewed as a knowledge representation formalism

## Detailed Summary

The article traces knowledge representation from its 1959 origins through five major eras. Allen Newell and Herbert Simon's GPS established the first data structures for AI planning. The 1960s-70s saw a logic vs. procedural knowledge debate between Stanford (formal logic) and MIT (ad hoc procedures), resolved through [[concepts/logic-programming]] and Prolog.

The 1970s brought [[entities/marvin-minsky]]'s frame theory — representing knowledge as stereotypical situations with slots, classes, and inheritance. The 1980s integrated frames with rule-based [[concepts/expert-systems]], exemplified by Intellicorp's KEE (1983). KL-ONE introduced rigorous formal semantics to frame languages, spawning description logics that would eventually underpin [[concepts/semantic-web]] standards like OWL.

[[entities/doug-lenat]]'s [[entities/cyc-project]] represented the most ambitious attempt at comprehensive common-sense KR. The 2000s saw KR techniques exported to the web via RDF and OWL.

Brian C. Smith's 1985 Knowledge Representation Hypothesis and Randall Davis's 1993 Five Roles framework provide the field's theoretical foundations. Ron Brachman's seven characteristics (1985) define the design space: primitives, meta-representation, incompleteness, definitions vs. facts, non-monotonic reasoning, expressive adequacy, and reasoning efficiency.

Tom Gruber's insight that "every ontology is a treaty" captures the social dimension of knowledge engineering.

## Notable Quotes
> "Every ontology is a treaty — a social agreement among people with common motive in sharing." — Tom Gruber

> "Any mechanically embodied intelligent process will be comprised of structural ingredients that we as external observers naturally take to represent a propositional account of the knowledge that the overall process exhibits." — Brian C. Smith (1985)
