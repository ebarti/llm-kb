## Key Points

- Luhmann maintained two separate slip boxes: bibliographic (source references) and main (personal ideas only)
- His workflow separated reading from reaction: read → reflect daily → rewrite in own words
- Folgezettel numbering (1, 1a, 1b, 1a1) captured the evolutionary path of thinking, not topic classification
- Fixed position ordering (Feste Stellordnung) rejected thematic classification entirely
- Hub Notes served as "highways" navigating 90,000 cards
- Luhmann described his system as a "communication partner" — an external mind for dialogue
- He built two complete systems: ZK I (~23,000 cards, law) and ZK II (~66,000 cards, sociology)
- Modern "fleeting/literature/permanent notes" terminology was NOT Luhmann's — it comes from Ahrens' 2017 interpretation

## Detailed Summary

This source corrects widespread misunderstandings about Luhmann's Zettelkasten by analyzing his actual practice rather than later interpretations. The most significant correction: Luhmann did not use the three-note-type system (fleeting, literature, permanent) popularized by Soenke Ahrens' "How to Take Smart Notes" (2017). His actual system was simpler — two physically separate slip boxes with a disciplined workflow.

The **bibliographic slip box** was purely for reference: citation details on the front, relevant page numbers and concepts on the back. The **main slip box** contained only ideas Luhmann had personally processed and reformulated in his own words. He did not highlight books — he considered highlighting "merely marking others' ideas." True thinking happened during the rewriting phase.

The **Folgezettel** (follow-up note) numbering system is often misunderstood as hierarchical classification. It was actually a record of the thinking process — where each new idea branched from existing ones. The numbering (1, 1a, 1a1, 1a1a) shows the evolutionary path of thought, not a taxonomy. Combined with **fixed position ordering** (each card permanently keeps its number), this created a system where ideas gained meaning through their network position rather than their categorical placement.

Perhaps most striking is that Luhmann built two entirely separate systems when he changed fields from law to sociology, rather than expanding the existing one.
