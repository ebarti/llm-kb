## Key Points

- US Copyright Office (May 2025): "some uses of copyrighted works for AI training will qualify as fair use, and some will not"
- Bartz v. Anthropic & Kadrey v. Meta (June 2025): ruled AI training as "highly transformative" and fair use
- Thomson Reuters v. ROSS (Feb 2025): fair use defense failed — headnotes were protectable
- Emerging consensus: general-purpose AI training is "highly transformative"
- 2026: cases involving OpenAI, Google expected; potential peak of AI copyright cases
- Litigation focus shifting from training data to AI outputs

## Detailed Summary

The legal landscape around AI training data is rapidly evolving. The two June 2025 decisions from the Northern District of California (Bartz v. Anthropic, Kadrey v. Meta) established that training general-purpose AI models on copyrighted works is "highly transformative" — a key factor in fair use analysis. However, these rulings are narrow and fact-specific, and the Thomson Reuters v. ROSS decision went the opposite direction.

The Copyright Office's 108-page report deliberately avoided a blanket rule, acknowledging that fair use is inherently case-by-case. This creates significant uncertainty for AI companies, particularly around: what types of copyrighted works are more/less protected, whether commercial vs. research use matters, and how closely an AI output must resemble training data to constitute infringement.

For 2026, the focus is shifting from whether training is permissible to whether AI outputs infringe — a potentially more dangerous liability surface for AI companies.
