## Key Points

- Memex = "memory" + "index" — a hypothetical electromechanical device described in Bush's 1945 "As We May Think" (The Atlantic)
- Key innovation: "associative trails" — user-created links between documents mimicking human mental association
- Directly inspired Douglas Engelbart (mouse, hypertext demo), Ted Nelson (coined "hypertext" in 1965), and influenced Berners-Lee's Web
- Bush's 1959 "Memex II" envisioned networked professional knowledge sharing
- 1967 "Memex Revisited" acknowledged that "selection, in the broad sense, is still a stone adze" — the retrieval problem persists
- DARPA's 2014 Memex program explicitly cited Bush; Microsoft's MyLifeBits (2003) attempted to fulfill the vision

## Detailed Summary

Vannevar Bush's 1945 essay "As We May Think" is the foundational document of personal knowledge management and tools for thought. Writing in The Atlantic as WWII ended, Bush — who had directed the U.S. Office of Scientific Research and Development — argued that the explosion of scientific knowledge had outstripped humanity's ability to find and use it. His proposed solution was the memex: a desk-sized device that would store an individual's entire library on microfilm, accessible via mechanical search, with the ability to create **associative trails** linking related documents.

The associative trail concept is the memex's most prescient feature. Rather than relying on alphabetical or hierarchical indexing (which Bush considered arbitrary and cognitively unnatural), users would forge personal paths through their knowledge, adding annotations and side trails. This directly prefigured hypertext, bidirectional linking, and the entire philosophy of networked thought.

The influence chain is direct and documented. **Douglas Engelbart** read the essay in 1945 and became "infected" with extending human knowledge interaction — leading to the mouse, hypertext, and the 1968 "Mother of All Demos." **Ted Nelson** coined "hypertext" in 1965 explicitly referencing Bush's associative trails. **Tim Berners-Lee** developed the Web independently but acknowledged Bush's validation of his ideas.

Bush continued refining the concept: Memex II (1959) envisioned networked professional societies sharing interconnected knowledge, and Memex Revisited (1967) acknowledged that despite hardware advances, the fundamental problem of intelligent selection remained unsolved — a problem that arguably only LLMs have begun to address 80 years later.

## Notable Quotes

> "An enlarged intimate supplement to his memory." — Bush describing the memex

> "Selection, in the broad sense, is still a stone adze." — Bush, 1967, on the persistence of the retrieval problem
