## Key Points
- Published July 1945 in The Atlantic; post-WWII call to redirect science toward understanding
- Proposed the Memex: microfilm-based device with associative trails between documents
- Key innovation: associative indexing vs. hierarchical classification — mirroring human memory
- Predicted hypertext, personal computers, the web, speech recognition, online encyclopedias
- Directly influenced Engelbart (mouse, hyperlink), Nelson (hypertext), and Berners-Lee (web)

## Detailed Summary

[[entities/vannevar-bush]]'s 1945 essay stands as the founding document of information science and personal knowledge management. Writing as WWII ended, Bush urged scientists to redirect from weapons toward tools for understanding.

The [[concepts/memex]] was his central proposal: a desk-sized electromechanical device using microfilm to store an individual's entire library of documents, books, and records. The revolutionary feature was **associative indexing** — creating connections between any items rather than filing them hierarchically. Bush called these connections "trails," which users could create, annotate, and share.

Bush's key quote — "wholly new forms of encyclopedias will appear, ready-made with a mesh of associative trails running through them" — essentially predicted Wikipedia and the hyperlinked web.

The essay diagnosed the "information explosion" that would dominate the rest of the century: scientific knowledge was accumulating faster than researchers could process it. Bush's solution was to "delegate routine mathematics to machines" so humans could focus on creative, intuitive work — a vision remarkably similar to the modern argument for AI-augmented knowledge work.

The essay's influence was enormous: [[entities/douglas-engelbart]] read it and began work that led to the mouse, word processor, and hyperlink. [[entities/ted-nelson]] extended Bush's associative trails into formal [[concepts/hypertext]] theory. Tim Berners-Lee's web inherited the spirit of associative linking.
