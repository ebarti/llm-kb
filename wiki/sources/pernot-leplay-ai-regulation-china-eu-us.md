## Key Points

- EU AI Act: risk-based categorization, prohibits social scoring and public facial recognition, Fundamental Rights Impact Assessments
- US: no federal comprehensive framework; sector-specific bills, voluntary guidelines, state initiatives (California SB 1047 vetoed)
- China: iterative regulation (GenAI rules drafted April 2023, finalized August 2023), "best effort" standards, copyright protection for AI-generated content
- China emphasizes "correct political direction" per Xi Jinping's directive
- EU risks "Brussels Effect" where its standards become global default
- The race to establish international AI norms remains "wide open"

## Detailed Summary

This comparative analysis maps three fundamentally different approaches to [[concepts/ai-regulation-landscape]]. The EU leads with the most comprehensive framework but struggles with regulatory agility — ChatGPT emerged after the AI Act's 2021 proposal, requiring substantial amendments. The US prioritizes innovation freedom but risks losing global influence through fragmentation. China's "develop hard, control tight" strategy enables rapid deployment while maintaining political control.

The key insight is that unlike privacy regulation (where GDPR became the global standard), AI regulation has no clear frontrunner. Each approach carries distinct tradeoffs between rights protection, innovation speed, and state control.
