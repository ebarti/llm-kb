## Key Points

- Knowledge commons: collectively owned and managed information resources, non-subtractible (use increases rather than depletes value)
- Two intellectual traditions: European (resisting intellectual enclosure) and American (commons as democratic spaces)
- Hess and Ostrom: knowledge requires institutional infrastructure to become truly shared
- Copyleft licensing as institutional mechanism for sustaining commons
- UNESCO: governance structure determines what remains commons vs. private enclosure

## Detailed Summary

The [[concepts/knowledge-commons]] concept encompasses collectively owned information resources with a unique property: unlike natural commons (fisheries, forests), use by one person does not reduce availability — and in fact **enhances value** for others. Wikipedia exemplifies this: each edit adds value accessible to all.

Charlotte Hess and [[entities/elinor-ostrom]] provided the theoretical foundation, adapting Ostrom's Nobel Prize-winning commons governance framework to knowledge. Their key insight: information does not automatically become shared knowledge — it requires "a complex set of institutions and practices" to enable internalization. This frames [[concepts/collaborative-knowledge-building]] as fundamentally an institutional design challenge, not just a technical one.

The governance mechanisms include copyleft licensing (GPL, Creative Commons share-alike) which institutionalizes the commons by requiring derivative works to remain open. Production models draw on Robert K. Merton's concept of "knowledge communism" — contributors surrender IP rights for recognition and esteem, exactly the incentive structure powering [[concepts/wikipedia-knowledge-model]].

The contemporary challenge: AI complicates this model by extracting value from commons data at industrial scale while potentially degrading the commons through content homogenization and reduced human contribution — the paradox explored in [[sources/cip-generative-ai-digital-commons]].
