## Overview

The world's three largest AI powers have adopted fundamentally different regulatory philosophies. Unlike data privacy — where GDPR became the global standard through the [[concepts/brussels-effect]] — AI regulation has no clear frontrunner. The outcome of this three-way competition will determine where AI innovation concentrates and how AI systems are deployed globally.

## Comparison Table

| Dimension | EU | US | China |
|-----------|----|----|-------|
| **Framework** | Single comprehensive law (AI Act) | State patchwork + sector bills | Iterative targeted regulations |
| **Philosophy** | Rights protection first | Innovation first | Strategic dominance + political control |
| **Enforcement** | Binding; up to EUR 35M or 7% of global revenue | Mostly voluntary guidelines | Political + legal enforcement |
| **Key Law** | AI Act (Aug 2026 full enforcement) | No federal law; state laws (IL, CO, CA) | GenAI rules, algorithm registries |
| **Speed** | Slow (multi-year legislative process) | Medium (executive orders, state bills) | Fast (draft to final in months) |
| **Prohibitions** | Social scoring, public facial recognition | None at federal level | Content censorship requirements |
| **Open Source** | Limited exemptions, obligations for risky models | No specific framework | "Best effort" standards for all |
| **AI Registration** | Required for high-risk systems | Not required | Algorithm registry mandated |
| **Copyright (AI output)** | Challenging for developers | Claims largely dismissed | Protected |
| **Global Strategy** | Export via "Brussels Effect" | Market dominance | Standard-setting forums |
| **Foundation Models** | Transparency obligations | No specific rules | Content alignment requirements |
| **Implementation Risk** | Standards/guidance delayed; member states behind | Compliance chaos from state fragmentation | Regulatory gray areas |

## Detailed Analysis

### EU: Comprehensive but Potentially Slow

The [[entities/eu-ai-act]] is the most ambitious attempt to regulate AI comprehensively. Its risk-tiered approach (unacceptable, high-risk, limited-risk, minimal-risk) provides clear structure, and Fundamental Rights Impact Assessments embed human rights into the design process. However, the Act's biggest vulnerability is speed: ChatGPT emerged after the 2021 proposal, requiring substantial amendments. Harmonized technical standards may not be ready by the August 2026 deadline.

### US: Innovation-Friendly but Fragmented

The US has no federal AI framework. The Biden executive order established voluntary guidelines, many of which the Trump administration rolled back. State laws are filling the vacuum, creating a patchwork: Illinois requires AI disclosure in hiring (January 2026), Colorado launches a comprehensive AI Act (June 2026), and California mandates content labeling (August 2026). California's SB 1047 (comprehensive safety requirements) was vetoed, signaling resistance to broad regulation.

### China: Agile but Politically Constrained

China's "develop hard, control tight" strategy enables rapid regulatory response — generative AI rules went from draft to final in four months. The approach favors "best effort" standards rather than strict obligations, encouraging adoption. However, the emphasis on "correct political direction" and state censorship requirements limit the framework's international applicability.

## When to Prefer Each Approach

| Scenario | Best Approach | Why |
|----------|--------------|-----|
| Protecting citizens' rights | EU | Most comprehensive rights framework |
| Maximizing innovation speed | US | Least regulatory burden |
| Rapid policy response to new tech | China | Fastest regulatory cycle |
| Global standard-setting | EU (aspiration) | Most structured and exportable |
| National security integration | China/US | Both integrate AI into state security |
| Open-source ecosystem development | US | Least restrictive for open models |

## Key Insight

The article notes that "the race to establish international norms to solve the AI dilemma is still wide open" ([[sources/pernot-leplay-ai-regulation-china-eu-us]]). This contrasts sharply with privacy regulation, where the EU decisively won the standards race. The absence of a clear global leader creates an unstable equilibrium where companies must navigate three divergent frameworks simultaneously, and where China's agility may give it an advantage in standard-setting forums where the US is disengaged.
