## Overview

Douglas Carl Engelbart (1925-2013) was an American engineer and inventor who pioneered interactive computing, the computer mouse, hyperlinks, windowed interfaces, and collaborative real-time editing. His work at the Stanford Research Institute's Augmentation Research Center (ARC) was motivated by his 1962 framework "Augmenting Human Intellect."

## Key Contributions

### "Augmenting Human Intellect: A Conceptual Framework" (1962)
Engelbart's foundational paper argued that computers should amplify human intellectual capabilities. His "bootstrapping" approach proposed that users and computers would co-evolve, each adapting to the other's improving capabilities.

### The NLS (oNLine System)
With ARPA, NASA, and Air Force funding, Engelbart built NLS, which introduced:
- The computer mouse
- Hyperlinks
- Windowed graphical user interface
- Real-time collaborative editing
- Version control
- Interactive text manipulation

### "The Mother of All Demos" (December 9, 1968)
A 90-minute live demonstration at the Fall Joint Computer Conference in San Francisco where Engelbart and his team showcased NLS features including real-time video conferencing, collaborative editing, hyperlinked documents, and the mouse — all in an era when batch processing on punch cards was the norm.

This demo is widely considered the most important technology demonstration in computing history.

### Influence from Bush
Engelbart encountered [[entities/vannevar-bush]]'s "As We May Think" shortly after its 1945 publication. The [[concepts/memex]] concept became central to his research vision, leading directly to the invention of hyperlinks and the mouse as enabling technologies for associative knowledge navigation.

## Legacy

Engelbart's work directly led to:
- Xerox PARC's Alto (1973) -> Apple Macintosh (1984) -> modern GUIs
- The World Wide Web's hyperlink model
- Google Docs and collaborative editing tools
- Modern [[concepts/personal-knowledge-management]] tools

His vision of "augmenting human intellect" — computers as tools for amplifying human thinking rather than replacing it — is the philosophical foundation of [[concepts/llm-knowledge-base]] systems.
