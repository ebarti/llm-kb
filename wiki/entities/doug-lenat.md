## Overview

Douglas Bruce Lenat (September 13, 1950 - August 31, 2023) was an American computer scientist and AI researcher who founded and led the [[entities/cyc-project]], the most ambitious attempt to hand-encode human common-sense knowledge into a machine-readable form. He served as CEO of Cycorp, Inc. in Austin, Texas, until his death from bile duct cancer.

## Key Contributions

### AM (Automated Mathematician)
Lenat's early program discovered mathematical theorems, some mirroring work by prodigy Srinivasa Ramanujan. It demonstrated that heuristic discovery could produce genuinely novel mathematical insights.

### EURISKO
Built on AM, EURISKO could modify its own heuristics. It famously won a Traveller naval wargame tournament through unconventional strategies (designing fleets of slow, heavily armored ships) that exploited loopholes in the rules.

### The Cyc Project (1984-2023)
Lenat's life work. Started at MCC in 1984, it aimed to encode the millions of pieces of common-sense knowledge humans take for granted. By 2017: 1.5 million terms, 24.5 million assertions, 2,000 person-years of effort.

### RLL (Representation Language Language)
Developed with Russell Greiner at Stanford (1979-1980), RLL became the foundation for CycL, the Cyc knowledge representation language.

### Final Paper: Cyc + LLM Integration
Before his death, Lenat proposed combining Cyc's auditable reasoning chains with LLMs' natural language fluency and broad knowledge — a vision aligned with the [[concepts/neural-symbolic-integration]] trend. He recognized that LLMs possess breadth but lack consistency, while Cyc offers formal reasoning without linguistic facility.

## Key Quotes

> "Sometimes the veneer of intelligence is not enough."

> "By 1999 no one would even think about having a computer that doesn't have Cyc running on it." (1989 prediction with R.V. Guha — proved spectacularly wrong)

## Assessment

Pedro Domingos called Cyc a "catastrophic failure." Gary Marcus saw it as a fundamentally different approach from deep learning that addressed real problems. The truth is nuanced: Cyc demonstrated both the necessity and the insufficiency of explicit knowledge encoding — a lesson directly relevant to modern [[concepts/llm-knowledge-base]] systems.
